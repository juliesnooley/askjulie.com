Q-001Do I have to tell anyone I'm homeschooling in California?Getting started

Yes — not in the sense of asking permission, but in the sense that your child has to be legally enrolled somewhere. California doesn't have a "homeschool law"; it has four doors, and you have to be standing in one of them.

  • File your own private school affidavit (PSA). You become a small private school. Most independence, most paperwork on you.
  • Enroll in a private school satellite program (PSP). An umbrella private school files the affidavit and holds the records; you teach. Costs money, removes admin.
  • Public charter or district independent study. Public funding and often a materials allowance, in exchange for public school reporting, assignments, and oversight.
  • Hire a credentialed tutor. Legal, rarely practical.

Nobody grants you approval and nobody inspects you on the way in. What matters is that if a district ever asks, you can name your door and produce the records that go with it.

Compulsory education in California runs from age 6 to 18. Below 6, none of this applies yet — you can simply not enroll.

Q-002What records do I actually have to keep?Legal & records

If you've filed your own affidavit, the Education Code asks a private school for a short, specific list. It is much less than people fear, and much more specific than people assume.

  • An attendance register — days of instruction, kept as you go, not reconstructed in June.
  • A course of study — what subjects each child is being taught this year.
  • A faculty list with qualifications — that's you. No credential required.
  • Instruction in English, in the branches of study taught in public schools.

That's the legal floor. The floor is not the goal. I keep more than that — work samples, a running course description file, reading lists — not because anyone requires it, but because a transcript is far easier to build from records than from memory, and memory is where most families lose four years of evidence.

Keep it boring and contemporaneous. One spreadsheet, updated weekly, beats a beautiful binder assembled in a panic.

Q-003When is the affidavit due, and what happens if I miss it?Legal & records

The filing window is October 1–15, annually, online with the California Department of Education. It's short, it's the same fortnight every year, and it's the single date I'd put in your calendar today with a two-week warning on it.

Miss it and the sky does not fall — but on paper you have a child of compulsory school age enrolled nowhere, and that is the situation you do not want to be explaining to a district. The fix is to file as soon as you realize, or to enroll in a satellite program that files on your behalf.

Starting mid-year is a different case entirely and is genuinely fine; the affidavit is an annual snapshot, not a starting gun.

Filing is free. Anyone charging you a fee to file an affidavit is selling you a satellite program — which may be worth it, but know which one you're buying.

Q-004Should I file my own affidavit or join a satellite program?Getting started

This is the most common question I get, and the honest answer is that it depends on one thing: whether administration is a burden to you or a comfort.

File your own if you are organized, want zero oversight, want it free, and are comfortable being the only person responsible for your records. This is where I ended up.

Join a satellite program if you want someone else holding the records, a transcript service at the end, a community, and a person to call when you're unsure. You'll pay somewhere in the low hundreds to low thousands per year, and you'll accept their requirements — some ask for work samples or attendance reporting on their schedule.

Neither is more legitimate than the other. What actually predicts a good year is whether the records get kept, and you should pick the option under which you will keep them.

Q-005What does homeschooling actually cost?Money

Far less than people assume, and in a distribution that surprises them. Here's a real per-child year at the middle of my range — one elementary-age child, filing our own affidavit, no satellite program.

Two things to notice. The legal part is free. And the largest line isn't curriculum — it's activities, which are optional and where every family's number diverges. A determined library-and-secondhand year runs under $400 a child; a satellite program with outsourced high school classes runs several thousand.

The cost nobody puts in the spreadsheet is the income you don't earn. That one is real, it's usually the largest number on the page, and it deserves to be decided deliberately rather than discovered later.

Q-006How do I make a transcript a college will actually accept?High school

A homeschool transcript is a one-page document you produce yourself, and admissions offices see them constantly. It is not exotic. It has to be legible and consistent, which is a lower bar than "impressive."

  • Course title, year taken, credits, grade — four columns, by year.
  • One credit = roughly a year-long course. Be consistent about how you decided that and write the rule down.
  • A short course-description document to accompany it: two or three sentences per course, plus texts used.
  • Your grading scale, stated plainly at the bottom. Yes, you may assign grades. Yes, you should be willing to defend them.

For the UC and CSU systems, homeschooled applicants generally come in through the exam and community college routes rather than the A–G course list, since A–G approval attaches to accredited course providers. Community college coursework during high school is the single strongest thing you can put on the page — it's externally graded, transferable, and settles the credibility question outright.

Build the transcript in ninth grade and update it each June. Reconstructing four years at once is the most avoidable misery in homeschooling.

Q-007How many hours a day should we actually be doing?Day to day

Less than you think, and the gap between the honest answer and the expected one is why so many first years feel like failures.

For a six- to eight-year-old, focused academic work is realistically 60 to 90 minutes. Middle years, two to three hours. High school approaches a full workload, four to six hours, because the material genuinely demands it. Everything past that in the early years is either handwriting practice or theater.

A school day is long because it's moving thirty children through a building. You're moving one, at their pace, with no transitions and no waiting. The efficiency is not a shortcut you're getting away with — it's the entire mechanism.

Track days of instruction, not hours. It's what the register asks for, and it stops you grading yourself against a clock that was never measuring your situation.

Q-008Can I homeschool with a toddler in the house?Day to day

Yes, and it's the single most underestimated logistical problem in homeschooling — far harder than any academic question on this page.

What worked here: do the one subject that genuinely requires my full attention during the toddler's nap, and protect that window like a meeting. Everything else — reading aloud, history, anything that tolerates interruption — happens with the toddler in the room and given a job. A basket of things only allowed out during school hours buys more than any curriculum choice.

The older children also carry some of it, in short defined shifts, not as unpaid childcare. Twenty minutes, named in advance, is sustainable. "Watch your brother while I teach" is not.

The year will be less tidy than the plan. The plan should be written knowing that.

Q-009My child is behind in math. Do I hold them back?Curriculum

No — because "behind" is a grade-level concept, and grade levels are an artifact of grouping thirty children by birthday. You don't have that constraint, and adopting its vocabulary imports a problem you were free of.

What's actually true is narrower and more useful: there's a specific skill that isn't solid, and everything downstream of it is wobbling. Almost always it's fractions, place value, or unmemorized multiplication facts. Find the exact rung, go back to it, and rebuild from there — even if that means a nine-year-old spending six weeks on something labeled second grade.

Six weeks of that is faster than two years of accumulating gaps. This is the closest thing homeschooling has to a superpower and most families are too embarrassed to use it.

If the gap doesn't close with focused work over a term, that's a signal worth taking to an evaluation rather than more curriculum.

Q-010Charter school funds — is the money worth it?Money

We did one charter year and didn't repeat it, so take this as one family's arithmetic rather than a verdict.

The offer is real: a per-child instructional funds allowance, often in the high hundreds to low thousands, spendable on approved vendors, classes, and materials. For a family stretched thin, that is not nothing.

What you trade is that your child is enrolled in public school. That means an assigned education specialist, work samples submitted on their calendar, state testing, curriculum restricted to secular approved vendors, and the funds themselves being district property with rules about what they touch. Some families find the structure genuinely supportive. I found I was doing more paperwork for someone else's compliance than I'd ever done for my own.

Ask one question before enrolling: how many work samples per month, and who decides if they're sufficient? The answer tells you what the money actually costs.
